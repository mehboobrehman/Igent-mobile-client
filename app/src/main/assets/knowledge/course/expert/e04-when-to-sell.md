## The Big Idea

Buying gets all the attention, but selling decides your results. Most investors sell for the wrong reasons: fear after a fall, or impatience after a small gain. A good sale has a clear reason you could write down, just like a good buy.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Five good reasons to sell

| Reason | What it looks like | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Thesis broken** | The reason you bought is no longer true: profits collapse, a governance scandal, a policy change that hurts the business for years | "Did my line 5 (what proves me wrong) happen?" |
| **2. Better opportunity** | A clearly better idea needs money, and you have no spare cash | "Is the new idea better after tax and costs of switching?" |
| **3. Overvaluation** | The price now runs far ahead of any reasonable value; future returns look poor | "At today's price, would I buy this now?" |
| **4. Rebalancing** | A position or asset class has grown past your cap or band | "Is it beyond my limit?" |
| **5. Needing the money** | A goal is close: fees, a wedding, a house | "When do I need this, and can it handle a fall before then?" |

The test "would I buy this today at this price?" is powerful. If the answer is a clear no, and you hold it only because you already own it, that is a sign.

### Bad reasons to sell (noise)

**Noise** is information that moves prices but does not change the business. Examples:

- The KSE-100 fell 3% today on political headlines.
- A WhatsApp group says "exit now".
- The price is below what you paid, and you are tired of looking at red.
- The price is up 15% and you want to "lock in" profit, even though the thesis is still strong and the position is within your cap.

When noise hits, re-read your thesis. If nothing in it broke, a price move alone is not a reason.

### Remember: the market does not know your buy price

**Anchoring** is judging a stock by the price you paid. "I will sell when it gets back to Rs 200" ties your decision to a number that means nothing to the business.

### Selling in parts

Selling is rarely all-or-nothing. **Trimming** means selling part of a position. It is useful when:

- A winner has grown past your cap, but the thesis still holds.
- The price looks rich but not crazy.
- You are unsure and want to reduce risk.

A simple approach: sell a third now, a third if the price runs further, and keep the rest while the thesis holds. If the thesis is truly broken, though, do not drag it out. Exit.

### Tax and timing

- **Capital gains tax (CGT):** when you sell listed shares at a gain, NCCPL works out and deducts CGT through your broker. Fund redemptions have CGT deducted by the AMC. The rate depends on your filer status and, under some laws, on when you bought and how long you held. Rules change with budgets, so check current rates (FBR, NCCPL, or ask Igent's Chat) before a big sale.
- **Netting:** gains and losses on securities within the same tax year are generally netted. Carry-forward of losses is limited; check current rules.
- **Costs:** brokerage and other charges apply on each sale. Selling small amounts very often adds up.
- **Dividends:** if you sell before the **book closure** (the cut-off for who gets a dividend), you will not get that dividend. But on the ex-dividend day the price usually falls by roughly the dividend, so do not twist a good decision around it.

Tax matters, but never let it keep you in a broken idea.

### Your sell checklist

```text
Which reason applies?  [ ] Thesis broken  [ ] Better idea  [ ] Overvalued
                       [ ] Over cap/band  [ ] Need the money  [ ] None (noise -> hold)
Would I buy it today at this price?  yes / no
Sell all or part? ______ (part if thesis holds, all if broken)
Estimated CGT and costs: Rs ______ (check current rates)
Where does the money go next? ______
Written in my journal on: ______
```

## A Pakistani Example

*All numbers are made-up round figures for teaching, not real data or advice.*

Kamran owns 1,000 shares of **Lucky Cement (LUCK)**, bought at Rs 150 in this example. The price is now Rs 260. His portfolio has grown, and LUCK is now 18% of it. His own cap is 12%.

**Step 1: Which reason?** He re-reads his thesis: cement demand recovering, costs easing. The latest results still support it. So the thesis is not broken. But the position is over his cap. Reason 4 applies: rebalancing.

**Step 2: All or part?** Thesis holds, so he trims. To bring LUCK from 18% back near 12%, he needs to sell about a third. He rounds up and sells 400 shares, leaving a little room below the cap.

**Step 3: Tax and costs.**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale value: 400 x Rs 260 | Rs 104,000 |
| Cost of those shares: 400 x Rs 150 | Rs 60,000 |
| Gain | Rs 44,000 |
| CGT at an *assumed* 15% (check the real rate) | Rs 6,600 |
| Trading costs, assumed | about Rs 300 |
| Gain kept after tax and costs | about Rs 37,100 |

**Step 4: Where does it go?** His income funds are below target, so the Rs 104,000 (less tax and costs) goes there. He keeps 600 shares while the thesis holds.

Two months later, LUCK drops 12% in a week on market-wide selling. His friends panic. Kamran checks his thesis: demand still fine, margins within his range. Nothing in line 5 has happened, so he holds. That is the difference between a reason and noise.

## Common Mistakes

- **Panic selling on noise** after a market-wide fall, then missing the recovery.
- **Holding losers to "get back to even"** when the thesis is clearly broken.
- **Selling winners too early** just because they are up, while the story is still intact.
- **Letting tax freeze you** in a bad position.
- **All-or-nothing thinking** when trimming would fit better.

## Key Takeaways

- Good reasons: thesis broken, better opportunity, overvaluation, rebalancing, needing the money.
- Price noise alone is not a reason. Re-read the thesis instead.
- Ask "would I buy this today at this price?" and ignore your buy price.
- Trim in parts when the thesis holds; exit fully when it is broken.
- Check current CGT and costs, but never let tax keep you in a broken idea.
