## The Big Idea

A great company can be a bad investment if you pay too much. A weak company can be a good one if the price is low enough. **Valuation** means comparing the price you pay with what you get: profits, assets and dividends. But a low number is a question, not an answer. Sometimes a stock is cheap because the market knows something bad.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### P/E: how many years of profit you are paying for

**P/E** (price-to-earnings) = share price ÷ EPS.

If a share costs Rs 100 and EPS is Rs 10, the P/E is 10. Roughly, you are paying 10 years of current profit. A lower P/E looks cheaper, but only if profits will hold up.

- **Trailing P/E** uses the last 12 months of EPS.
- **Forward P/E** uses expected EPS for the next year, which is a guess.
- If a company made a loss, P/E means nothing. Use other measures.

### Earnings yield: P/E turned upside down

**Earnings yield** = EPS ÷ price = 1 ÷ P/E. A P/E of 10 is an earnings yield of 10%. A P/E of 20 is 5%.

This is handy because you can compare it with the **safe rate**: what you could earn with almost no risk, such as T-bills, a money market fund or a bank deposit. A share carries risk, so it should offer a clear extra return over the safe rate. When safe rates are high, shares need to be cheaper to compete. When rates fall, investors accept higher P/Es. That is one reason rate cuts often lift the whole market.

### P/B: price compared with book value

**Book value per share** (BVPS) = equity ÷ shares. **P/B** = price ÷ BVPS.

P/B matters most for banks and asset-heavy firms, where the assets are mostly money and loans. A P/B below 1 means the market values the company below its accounts. Ask why: bad loans, weak returns, or simply a gloomy market?

A useful partner is **ROE** (return on equity) = net profit ÷ equity. A bank earning a high ROE deserves a higher P/B than one earning a low ROE.

### Dividend yield and payout ratio

**Dividend yield** = dividends per share over the last 12 months ÷ price.

**Payout ratio** = dividend per share ÷ EPS. It shows how much of profit is paid out.

| Payout | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| Low (say under 40%) | Company keeps cash to grow or repay debt |
| Middle | Balanced |
| Very high (near or above 100%) | Little room for bad years. Dividend may be cut |

Remember that dividend tax is deducted before you get the cash, so compare after-tax yields. Check current tax rules with Igent or FBR.

### Compare like with like

A ratio alone means little. Compare it two ways:

1. **Sector peers.** A bank's P/E should be compared with other banks, not with a tech company. Different sectors normally trade at different levels.
2. **Its own history.** Is the P/E low compared with the company's usual range?

**Cyclical** companies (cement, autos, steel) need care. Their P/E looks lowest at the peak of the cycle, when profits are at a high that will not last, and highest at the bottom. Think about average profits across a full cycle.

### The value trap

A **value trap** is a stock that looks cheap and stays cheap, or gets cheaper, because its profits keep falling. Warning signs:

- Profits are falling, not just having one bad quarter.
- The problem is permanent: a lost contract, heavy debt, a broken business model.
- High dividend yield, but cash flow cannot support it.
- Profit stuck in receivables that may never be collected.
- Weak governance or a qualified audit report.

Simple test: write the bear case in one line. Then ask, "Is this problem temporary or permanent?"

## A Pakistani Example

Two imaginary banks, all numbers made up for learning:

| | Bank A | Bank B |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Rs 120 | Rs 60 |
| EPS | Rs 20 | Rs 15 |
| Dividend per share | Rs 10 | Rs 13 |
| Book value per share | Rs 100 | Rs 100 |

Work it out:

- **Bank A:** P/E = 120 ÷ 20 = 6. Earnings yield ≈ 16.7%. P/B = 1.2. Dividend yield = 10 ÷ 120 = 8.3%. Payout = 10 ÷ 20 = 50%. ROE ≈ 20%.
- **Bank B:** P/E = 60 ÷ 15 = 4. Earnings yield = 25%. P/B = 0.6. Dividend yield = 13 ÷ 60 = 21.7%. Payout = 13 ÷ 15 = 87%. ROE ≈ 15%.

Bank B looks far cheaper on every number. But a 21.7% yield is a loud signal. Why would the market price it so low? Maybe bad loans are rising, maybe the dividend will be cut, maybe a large one-off boosted EPS. Bank A earns a higher ROE and pays a dividend it can easily afford.

Neither is automatically right. Bank B could be a bargain if its problem is temporary. But you need to know *why* it is cheap before you buy. Say the safe rate were 10% after tax (an example, not today's rate). Bank A's 16.7% earnings yield gives a decent margin. Bank B's 25% looks huge, but only if that EPS is real and repeatable.

This is education, not a recommendation to buy any bank.

## Common Mistakes

- Buying because "P/E is only 4" without asking why.
- Comparing P/Es across very different sectors.
- Judging a cyclical company on its peak-year profit.
- Chasing the highest dividend yield without checking payout ratio and cash flow.
- Forgetting tax and costs when comparing with the safe rate.

## Key Takeaways

- P/E = price ÷ EPS. Earnings yield = 1 ÷ P/E, which you can compare with the safe rate.
- Use P/B and ROE for banks and asset-heavy companies.
- Dividend yield needs a sensible payout ratio and real cash behind it.
- Compare with sector peers and the company's own history.
- Cheap for a reason is common. Find the reason first.
