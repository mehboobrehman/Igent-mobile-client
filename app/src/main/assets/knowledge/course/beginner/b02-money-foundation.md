## The Big Idea

A house needs a strong foundation before you add floors. Your money is the same. Before you invest, you need a budget, an emergency fund, and a plan for expensive debt. Skip these and one bad month can force you to sell your investments at the worst time.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Step 1: A simple budget

A **budget** is a plan for where your monthly income goes. It does not have to be fancy. One popular example is the **50/30/20** split:

| Part | Share | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Needs | 50% | Rent, food, bills, school fees, loan instalments |
| Wants | 30% | Eating out, shopping, trips, new phone |
| Saving and debt | 20% | Emergency fund, investing, paying off loans |

This is an example, not a rule. A family with high rent or many dependants may only save 5% or 10% at first. That is fine. The habit matters more than the number.

A useful trick is **pay yourself first**. On salary day, move your saving amount out before you start spending. What is left is yours to spend freely. Many banks and fund companies let you set this up automatically.

Before you set a budget, track your spending for a month or two. Most people are surprised by how much goes on small daily things like snacks, rides and top-ups. Also remember big yearly costs, such as Eid, school admissions or weddings. Divide them by 12 and save for them every month.

### Step 2: The emergency fund

An **emergency fund** is money kept aside only for real surprises: a job loss, a medical bill, a car or motorbike repair. A common guideline is **3 to 6 months of essential expenses**. Essential means what you truly need to live, not your full lifestyle.

Keep more (closer to 6 months, or even beyond) if:
- your income is irregular, like freelance or business income,
- you are the only earner in the family,
- you have children or parents depending on you.

**Where to keep it:** somewhere safe and quick to reach, such as a bank savings account, a money market fund, or a short-term National Savings product you can encash. We cover these in later lessons. **Not** in shares, gold or long-lock certificates. Their value can be down, or your money can be stuck, exactly when you need it.

### Step 3: Pay off expensive debt first

Here is a powerful idea: **paying off a loan earns you a guaranteed return equal to its interest rate.**

Say your credit card charges 30% a year on unpaid balances (an example; check your own statement). If you pay off Rs 100,000 of it, you save Rs 30,000 a year in interest, guaranteed. It is very hard to find any investment that reliably earns 30% a year. So paying the card off first is the smart move.

| Debt type | Usually | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Credit card balance | Very expensive | Pay off first, then pay in full each month |
| Personal loan | Expensive | Pay down early if charges allow |
| Buy now, pay later | Feels free, but late fees add up | Count it as debt |
| Home finance | Long term, lower cost | Keep paying normally |

Pay the highest-rate debt first while paying at least the minimum on the others. And one firm rule: **never borrow money to invest in shares.**

### Step 4: Never invest money you need soon

If you need money within about a year (next year's fees, a wedding, Umrah), keep it in safe options. Markets can fall 20% or 30% in a bad year. A fall is only a problem if you are forced to sell, and needing the money soon is what forces you.

## A Pakistani Example

Bilal works in Lahore and earns Rs 80,000 a month. Here is his starting point:

- Essential spending (rent share, food, bills, transport, mobile package): Rs 50,000 a month.
- Credit card balance: Rs 60,000, being charged a high rate.
- He is also in a family **committee (BC)**, putting in Rs 5,000 a month.

**His plan:**
1. **Budget:** Rs 50,000 needs, Rs 5,000 for the BC, Rs 9,000 wants, and Rs 16,000 saving and debt (that is 20% of Rs 80,000).
2. **Card first:** he puts Rs 16,000 a month toward the card. In about 4 months it is gone, and he stops paying that expensive interest.
3. **Emergency fund next:** 3 months of essentials = 3 x Rs 50,000 = **Rs 150,000**. 6 months would be Rs 300,000. Since he is a salaried single earner, he aims for 4 months, Rs 200,000. When his BC payout of Rs 60,000 arrives, he puts all of it into this fund. That plus Rs 16,000 a month gets him to Rs 200,000 in about 9 months.
4. **Then investing:** only after that does he start investing regularly.

A note on the BC: a committee is a great saving discipline, but it pays no profit, and it depends fully on trust in the organiser and members. Treat it as a saving tool, not an investment.

## Common Mistakes

- Investing in shares before having any emergency fund.
- Counting your full lifestyle spending, so the target feels impossible. Use essentials only.
- Keeping the emergency fund in shares or gold, which may be down when you need it.
- Investing while carrying an expensive credit card balance.
- Borrowing to invest because a friend says a share "will surely double".

## Key Takeaways

- Budget first. Pay yourself first on salary day.
- Build an emergency fund of about 3 to 6 months of essential expenses, more if your income is unstable.
- Paying off expensive debt is a guaranteed return equal to its rate.
- Money needed within about a year stays in safe places.
- A strong foundation lets you stay calm when markets fall.
