# KYC, AML and illegal practices to avoid

**General information only, not legal advice.** Laws and rules change. For your own case, check the official source or ask a licensed lawyer or adviser.

For spotting scams and making complaints, see guides/complaints-and-safety. For opening accounts, see basics/accounts.

## Why everyone asks for KYC
Banks, brokers, AMCs, insurers and CDNS must "know their customer" (KYC). This is also called customer due diligence (CDD). It is a legal duty under anti-money laundering rules, not a choice by the firm. SECP and SBP each issue AML/CFT regulations for the firms they supervise.

Expect to be asked for:
- **Identity:** CNIC (or NICOP/passport), photo, address, contact details.
- **Source of income and source of funds:** your job or business, and where the money for this investment came from. Keep salary slips, bank statements, sale deeds or gift papers handy.
- **Beneficial ownership:** whether you are investing for yourself or for someone else. The "beneficial owner" is the person who really owns or controls the money.
- **PEP questions:** whether you or a close family member or associate is a politically exposed person (PEP), such as a senior public official. PEPs are not banned. They get extra checks.
- **Risk profiling:** brokers and AMCs ask about your age, income, goals and risk tolerance. This helps them offer suitable products.

Answer truthfully. False KYC information can lead to account freezes and legal trouble. Update the firm when your address, phone, income or status changes.

## AML law and the FMU, in concept
- The **Anti-Money Laundering Act, 2010** makes money laundering an offence.
- It also sets up the **Financial Monitoring Unit (FMU)**, Pakistan's financial intelligence unit.
- Banks, brokers and other reporting firms send the FMU **suspicious transaction reports** and **currency transaction reports** for large cash dealings.
- The firm is not allowed to tell you it has filed a report.
- The FMU analyses reports and passes intelligence to investigating agencies.

## What can trigger extra checks
- Large or frequent cash deposits, or cash just below reporting levels (called "structuring").
- Money coming from third parties, or payouts asked for into someone else's account.
- Activity that does not match your stated income or profession.
- Fast in-and-out movement of money with little trading.
- Links to high-risk countries, or a name similar to a sanctioned person.
- Refusing to explain the source of funds.

Extra checks are routine. They do not mean you are accused of anything. Answer with documents.

## Never lend your name or account
- Keep every account **in your own name, funded from your own bank account**. PSX tells investors to open the account in their own name and to operate it themselves.
- Never let a friend, relative or "manager" trade through your account. Never trade in someone else's name to hide who owns the money.
- The **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 2017** targets property held in one person's name when another person paid for it and benefits from it. A "benamidar" includes a person who lends their name. Benami property can be confiscated. FBR enforces this law.
- The law has exceptions, for example some holdings in the name of a spouse or child paid from known income. The details are technical. Ask a lawyer before putting investments in another person's name.
- If someone else's money passes through your account, **you** may have to explain it to the tax office and the FMU.

## Insider trading and market manipulation
The **Securities Act, 2015** makes these offences:
- **Insider trading:** dealing in listed securities using price-sensitive information that is not public, or passing such "inside information" to others.
- **False trading and market rigging:** creating a false appearance of active trading or of a security's price.
- **Market manipulation:** trades that push a price up or down to trick others into buying or selling.
- **False or misleading statements:** spreading untrue information to make others trade.

What this looks like in real life:
- **Pump-and-dump:** a group buys a thin small-cap stock, hypes it, then sells to the people who rushed in.
- **Rumours:** fake "news" about mergers, contracts or dividends spread on social media.
- **WhatsApp/Telegram "tip" groups:** paid "VIP calls" from unlicensed people. They are often part of a pump-and-dump. Joining in can leave you holding losses, or even facing questions yourself.
- **Front-running:** someone who knows a big client order is coming trades ahead of it for their own profit.

PSX warns investors not to act on advice from unlicensed advisers or unregistered social media sources.

## Illegal deposit-taking, Ponzi and MLM schemes
- Taking deposits from the public without a licence is illegal. So are pyramid, Ponzi and referral (MLM) schemes. SECP cites the Companies Act, 2017 for this.
- Being registered as a company does **not** allow a firm to take deposits.
- SECP publishes warnings and press releases naming such schemes. It acts against them.
- Under PSX rules, brokers must display a notice in their offices. It says that nobody is allowed to take deposit money on fixed profits.
- **Red flags:** fixed monthly "profit", returns far above bank rates, payouts that depend on bringing in new members, and pressure to reinvest.

## Forex, CFD and offshore trading apps
- SECP has warned about online platforms offering forex, CFDs, options, commodities or foreign shares to Pakistanis without a Pakistani licence. Many are based offshore.
- If such a platform freezes your money or disappears, no Pakistani regulator can help you get it back.
- Sending money abroad for this may also breach foreign exchange rules under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947.
- Trade Pakistani shares only through SECP-licensed PSX brokers. Trade futures only through licensed PMEX brokers. Both lists are on the exchanges' websites.

## Crypto and virtual assets
Pakistan's legal and regulatory position on virtual assets has been changing. Igent does not state a status here. Before buying, selling or holding crypto, check the latest official position from SBP, SECP and the federal government. Use only platforms those authorities say are allowed.

## Power of attorney and letting a broker trade for you
- By default, a broker should trade only on **your instructions**. Instructions come by recorded phone line, written or electronic message, or in person with written acknowledgement. You get a contract note for every trade.
- PSX's investor guidance lists "authorising the securities broker or any of its employees to execute trades on behalf of the customer" as a practice to avoid.
- If you want a trusted person to place orders for you, PSX advises:
  - give the authority **in writing to the broker**;
  - keep a copy.
- A broker may use your margin assets for anything other than your trading only if you authorise it in writing, as the rules require.
- Managing someone else's portfolio with discretionary authority is a regulated activity. Only an SECP-licensed firm may do it, for example an AMC offering discretionary portfolio management.

**Safeguards if you ever give authority:**
- Limit it in scope and time. Never give blanket authority.
- Never give authority to move cash or shares out of your account.
- Never share passwords, PINs or OTPs, even with an authorised person.
- Keep CDC and NCCPL SMS/e-alerts on. Check every contract note and statement.
- Report unauthorised trades at once. PSX rules give you only a short time to object before a trade is treated as accepted.
- Cancel the authority in writing when you no longer need it.
