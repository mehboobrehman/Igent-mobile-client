## The Big Idea

News moves prices, but not the way most people think. Markets react to **surprises**: the gap between what happened and what people expected. By the time good news is on every TV channel, it is often already in the price. Learning to ask "was this expected?" and "is this source real?" will save you from buying tops and selling bottoms.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Priced in

**Priced in** means the market already expects something, so the share price already reflects it. Thousands of investors read the same news and act quickly. If everyone expects a company to report strong profits, buyers move in before the results. On results day, "strong profits" is not new.

The question is never "is this good news?" It is "**is this better or worse than expected?**"

### Buy the rumour, sell the news

A common pattern: a stock rises for weeks on hopes of good news (a big dividend, a rate cut, an IMF deal). When the news is confirmed, the price falls. Early buyers sell to lock in gains, and there is no fresh surprise left to push it higher. That is **"buy the rumour, sell the news"**. It does not happen every time, but it happens often enough that you should not rush in on announcement day.

### Big scheduled events

| Event | When | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Results season | After each quarter ends | Results vs analyst expectations, dividend, one-offs |
| SBP rate decision | On SBP's published calendar | Size of move vs expectations, and the statement's tone |
| Monthly CPI | Usually early each month | Higher or lower than expected |
| Federal budget | Usually June | Taxes on capital gains, dividends, banks, sector duties |
| IMF reviews | Through each programme | Staff-level agreement, conditions, Board approval |
| Elections | Roughly every five years | Uncertainty before, stability after |

**Results season** is when most companies announce quarterly results within a few weeks. A company can report higher profit and still fall, if investors expected even more.

**Rate decisions:** check T-bill yields and brokerage polls before the meeting. If SBP does exactly what was expected, the move may be small. The statement's hints about the future can matter more than the decision itself.

**Budget:** proposals are debated and changed before the Finance Act passes. Wait for the final law before acting on a proposal.

**Elections and political events:** markets dislike uncertainty. They often fall before and rally once a stable result is clear. Short protests are often looked through. Policy continuity and the IMF programme usually matter more than who wins.

### Noise vs signal

**Noise** is news that feels important but does not change a company's long-term value. **Signal** is news that does.

| Usually noise | Could be signal |
|---|---|
| A one-day market drop with no clear reason | A big change in profits, debt or contracts |
| A TV anchor's prediction | Official SBP, PBS, PSX or company announcements |
| "Sources say" social media posts | A change in gas price, tariff or tax law for that sector |
| A celebrity buying a stock | Loss of a key customer or licence |

Ask: "Will this change the company's profits or cash over the next few years?" If not, it is probably noise.

### Check the source

Rumours spread fast on WhatsApp and social media, and fake "news" is sometimes used to manipulate prices. Before acting:

1. Find the original source. For company news, check the **PSX Data Portal** company announcements. For rates, check **SBP**. For inflation, check **PBS**.
2. Check the date. Old news gets recycled.
3. Be extra careful with small, thinly traded stocks, where a rumour can move the price easily.
4. If a "news" message also tells you to buy now, be suspicious.

Igent's Latest News section and Chat can help you find and summarise news from its sources, but always check important facts at the official source.

## A Pakistani Example

Made-up numbers for an imaginary fertilizer company:

- Analysts expect a big final dividend. Over four weeks, the share rises from **Rs 100 to Rs 120** as people buy early.
- The company announces a dividend of **Rs 10**, exactly as expected.
- The next day the share slips to **Rs 116**, as early buyers take profits. Then, on the ex-date, the price drops by roughly the dividend amount, to about **Rs 106**.

Investor A bought at Rs 100 a month early. Investor B bought at Rs 120 on announcement day, chasing the "great news". Investor B is now sitting on a loss on the share price, even though the news was good. The dividend does not rescue them: dividend tax is deducted, and the price usually falls by about the dividend on the ex-date anyway. Lesson: by announcement day, the expected news was already in the price.

## Try It

Next time SBP has a monetary policy meeting, ask Igent in Chat beforehand: "What is the market expecting from the next SBP decision?" Write down your guess. After the decision, compare the actual move and watch how the KSE-100 tile on the Dashboard reacts. Was it a surprise or priced in?

## Common Mistakes

- Buying on the day good news is announced, after the price already ran up.
- Treating every headline as a reason to trade.
- Acting on a forwarded message without checking PSX, SBP or PBS.
- Trading on budget proposals that may change before they become law.
- Selling everything on political news that the market often looks through.

## Key Takeaways

- Prices react to surprises versus expectations, not to news itself.
- "Buy the rumour, sell the news" is common around expected events.
- Know the calendar: results season, SBP decisions, CPI, budget, IMF reviews.
- Separate noise from signal: will this change profits or cash for years?
- Always check the original source and the date before acting.
