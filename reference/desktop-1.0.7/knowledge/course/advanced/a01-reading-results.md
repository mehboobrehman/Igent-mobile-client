## The Big Idea

A share is a slice of a real business. The business reports how it is doing in its **financial results**: a set of numbers published every quarter and every year. You do not need to be an accountant. If you can read three short statements and ask "where did the profit come from, and did it turn into cash?", you are already ahead of most people who buy on tips.

## Explain It Like I'm New

Results come as three main statements. Think of them as three questions.

| Statement | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Income statement (profit and loss) | Did the business make money this period? |
| Balance sheet | What does it own, and what does it owe, on one date? |
| Cash flow statement | Did real cash come in or go out? |

### The income statement, top to bottom

Read it like a waterfall. Money comes in at the top, and costs are taken out step by step.

- **Sales** (also called **revenue**): money from selling goods or services. Is it growing compared with the same quarter last year?
- **Gross profit**: sales minus the direct cost of making the product (raw material, fuel, factory power).
- **Operating profit**: gross profit minus running costs like salaries, selling and admin. This is what the core business earns.
- **Finance cost**: interest paid on loans. It jumps when interest rates rise or debt grows.
- **Other income**: interest on cash, exchange gains, selling an old asset. Nice to have, but not the core business.
- **Net profit** (profit after tax): what is left for shareholders after everything, including tax.

A **margin** is a profit line divided by sales. **Gross margin** = gross profit ÷ sales. **Net margin** = net profit ÷ sales. Margins show pricing power. If sales rise but margins shrink, costs are eating the growth.

### EPS: profit per share

**EPS** (earnings per share) = net profit ÷ number of shares. It lets you compare profit with the share price, which you will do in the next lesson.

Two traps. First, Pakistani results often show **cumulative** figures, such as "nine months" EPS. That is three quarters added together, not one. Second, watch for **one-offs**: a land sale, a tax reversal, a big exchange gain. Remove them to get **core EPS**, the profit the business can repeat.

### The balance sheet: own, owe, and the rest

- **Assets**: what the company owns. Factories, stock, cash, and **receivables** (money customers still owe it).
- **Liabilities**: what it owes. The big one to watch is **debt** (bank loans and bonds).
- **Equity**: assets minus liabilities. This is the owners' share, also called **book value**.

A simple health check is **debt-to-equity** = total borrowings ÷ equity. High debt is fine in good times but painful when rates rise.

### The cash flow statement, in plain words

Profit is an accounting opinion. Cash is a fact. **Operating cash flow** is the cash the business actually collected from its operations. If profit keeps rising but operating cash flow is weak, ask why. Often the answer is receivables piling up: the company "sold" but has not been paid. In Pakistan this is common in power, oil and gas because of **circular debt** (government-linked buyers paying late down a chain).

**Free cash flow** = operating cash flow minus money spent on new plant and equipment. Dividends are paid from cash, not from profit.

### Quarterly vs annual, and where to find them

- Listed companies publish results every quarter, plus a full **annual report** with audited accounts and a directors' report explaining the numbers.
- Many companies use a July to June year. Others, such as banks, use January to December. Check before comparing.
- Compare a quarter with the **same quarter last year**, not the previous quarter, because many businesses are seasonal.
- Results appear as company announcements on the **PSX Data Portal** (dps.psx.com.pk). The company first announces a board meeting date; the board then approves the results and any dividend or bonus.

## A Pakistani Example

Here is a made-up quarter for an imaginary cement company, "Indus Cement". All numbers are illustrative.

| Line | This quarter | Same quarter last year |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Rs 10,000m | Rs 8,000m |
| Gross profit | Rs 2,500m | Rs 2,400m |
| Finance cost | Rs 600m | Rs 300m |
| Other income (one-off land sale) | Rs 900m | Rs 0 |
| Net profit | Rs 1,500m | Rs 1,200m |
| Shares | 300m | 300m |

Reported EPS = 1,500 ÷ 300 = **Rs 5**, up from Rs 4. A headline might say "profit up 25%!" Now look closer:

- Sales grew 25%, but gross margin fell from 30% (2,400 ÷ 8,000) to 25% (2,500 ÷ 10,000). Costs rose faster than prices.
- Finance cost doubled. Debt or rates went up.
- Rs 900m came from selling land. That will not happen every quarter. Take out that gain (and the tax on it, roughly) and core profit is probably *lower* than last year.

So the real story is "weaker quarter, flattered by a one-off". You found that in two minutes, without a degree.

## Common Mistakes

- Reading a nine-month cumulative EPS as if it were one quarter.
- Celebrating a profit jump without checking for one-off gains or tax reversals.
- Comparing with the previous quarter in a seasonal business, instead of the same quarter last year.
- Ignoring cash flow. Profit that never turns into cash cannot pay dividends.
- Forgetting that bonus shares change the share count, so old EPS must be adjusted.

## Key Takeaways

- Income statement = profit, balance sheet = own and owe, cash flow = real cash.
- Margins tell you more than sales growth alone.
- Use core EPS: remove one-off gains and losses.
- Watch debt and finance cost, especially when rates are high.
