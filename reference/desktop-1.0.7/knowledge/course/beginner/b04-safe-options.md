## The Big Idea

Before you take any risk, you should know what you can earn with almost **no** risk. In Pakistan that means bank deposits, National Savings schemes and government T-bills. The return on these is called the **safe rate**, and every other investment idea must beat it, after tax, by enough to be worth the extra risk.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### 1. Bank savings and term deposits

- A **savings account** (also called a PLS account) pays profit on your balance. You can withdraw any time.
- A **term deposit** (TDR or certificate) locks your money for a fixed period, like 1 month to 5 years, usually for a better rate. Breaking it early usually costs you profit.
- A **current account** pays no profit at all. Fine for daily spending, not for saving.

SBP sets a **minimum profit rate** that conventional banks must pay on many PKR savings accounts, linked to SBP's interest rates. Islamic banks share profit from their pools and announce an **expected** rate each month instead of a fixed one.

Your deposits are also protected by the **Deposit Protection Corporation (DPC)**, part of SBP, up to a set limit per depositor per bank. Check dpc.org.pk for the current limit.

### 2. National Savings schemes (CDNS)

The **Central Directorate of National Savings (CDNS)** sells savings products that are direct promises of the **Government of Pakistan**. That means no bank risk. You can invest at National Savings Centres, some bank branches or the CDNS app.

| Scheme (examples) | Tenor | How profit is paid |
|---|---|---|
| Short Term Savings Certificates | 3, 6 or 12 months | At maturity |
| Special Savings Certificates | 3 years | Every 6 months |
| Regular Income Certificates | 5 years | Monthly |
| Defence Savings Certificates | 10 years | Added up and paid at the end |
| Behbood Savings Certificates | 10 years | Monthly; only for senior citizens, widows and disabled persons |

Rates are set by the government and change often. A certificate's rate is fixed when you buy it. Watch out: encashing some certificates very early can earn you little or no profit. Get current rates from Igent's Dashboard (the Safe Rates card) or savings.gov.pk.

### 3. T-bills

A **T-bill (Market Treasury Bill)** is a short loan you give to the government, usually for 3, 6 or 12 months. It works in a neat way: you buy it at a **discount** and get the full **face value** back at the end. The difference is your profit.

For example (made-up numbers), you pay Rs 90,000 for a 12-month T-bill with a face value of Rs 100,000. A year later you get Rs 100,000. Profit: Rs 10,000.

Individuals can buy T-bills through a bank (using an **IPS account**, a special account that holds government securities for you) or through SBP's online channels. Many beginners find it easier to get the same thing through a **money market fund**, which we cover in the next lesson.

### What "safe" really means

These options are safe in one way: your **rupees** are very unlikely to disappear. They are not safe from **inflation**. If the safe rate after tax is below inflation, your buying power still shrinks. Safe means "low risk of loss", not "guaranteed to make you richer".

### After-tax: the number you actually keep

The government takes **withholding tax** on profit from banks, National Savings and T-bills before it reaches you. **Non-filers** (people not on FBR's tax list, see lesson 7) usually pay a much higher rate, often about double.

> After-tax return = rate x (1 - tax rate)

Example: a rate of 12% with 15% tax gives 12 x 0.85 = **10.2%**. Tax rates change with each budget, so check current figures before comparing.

### The safe rate as a yardstick

Here is the golden rule: **compare every idea with the safe rate.**

If National Savings pays you about 10% after tax with almost no risk, why buy a share expected to earn 11%? That extra 1% is too little for the risk of a 30% fall. A risky idea needs to beat the safe rate by a clear margin. Pick the safe option that matches your time: for a 1-year idea, compare with a 12-month T-bill; for money you may need any day, compare with a money market fund.

## A Pakistani Example

Ayesha has Rs 500,000 that she will not need for one year. She is a **filer**. Using made-up example rates:

| Option | Example rate | Example tax | After tax | Profit on Rs 500,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bank savings account | 10% | 20% | 8.0% | Rs 40,000 |
| 12-month National Savings certificate | 12% | 15% | 10.2% | Rs 51,000 |
| 12-month T-bill | 12% | 15% | 10.2% | Rs 51,000 |

If inflation that year were 9%, her real return would be about 1.2% on the certificate and about -1% in the savings account.

Now her friend suggests a share "that should give 11% this year". Ayesha compares: the share might give 11% before tax and costs, or it might fall 20%. The safe options give her about 10.2% with no drama. She decides the share is not worth it for one-year money.

All rates here are examples. Check real ones on the Dashboard before deciding.

## Common Mistakes

- Comparing returns before tax. Always compare what you keep.
- Thinking "safe" means "beats inflation". It may not.
- Breaking a certificate or term deposit early and losing the profit.
- Chasing "up to" bank rates that need a huge minimum balance.
- Taking a risky bet that barely beats the safe rate.

## Key Takeaways

- Bank deposits, National Savings and T-bills are Pakistan's main low-risk options.
- T-bills are bought at a discount and pay full face value at the end.
- Always convert to after-tax: rate x (1 - tax rate).
- The safe rate is the yardstick. Any risky idea must clearly beat it.
- Get current rates from the Dashboard, SBP or savings.gov.pk, never from memory.
