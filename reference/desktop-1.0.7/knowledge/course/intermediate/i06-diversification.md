## The Big Idea

Don't put all your eggs in one basket. **Diversification** means spreading your money so that one bad event cannot wreck everything. **Asset allocation** is deciding how much goes into each big basket, such as fixed income, shares and gold. Research has long suggested that this mix explains much of how a portfolio behaves, often more than which individual shares you pick.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Three levels of spreading

| Level | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset classes** | Different types of investment that behave differently. | Money market funds, National Savings, shares, gold. |
| **Sectors** | Different industries within the stock market. | Banks, cement, fertilizer, technology, pharma. |
| **Single companies** | Many companies, so no one decides your fate. | Ten companies rather than one. |

You need all three. Owning five banks is not diversification; it is one bet on banks made five times.

### Why sectors matter so much on PSX

The KSE-100 leans heavily on a few large sectors, historically banks, oil and gas exploration, fertilizer, cement and power. One policy change, such as an interest rate move or a gas pricing decision, can hit a whole sector at once. If all your shares come from one or two of these, a single headline can sink your whole portfolio.

### Correlation in simple words

**Correlation** describes how two investments move together.

- **High correlation**: they usually rise and fall together, like two cement companies.
- **Low correlation**: one's move tells you little about the other's.
- **Negative correlation**: one tends to rise when the other falls.

Diversification works best when you combine things with low correlation. In Pakistan, gold and dollar-linked assets have sometimes held up when the rupee weakened and local shares struggled, though no pattern is guaranteed. Money market funds barely move at all, which steadies the whole mix.

Picture a Lahore shopkeeper selling both umbrellas and sunglasses: he earns whatever the weather.

### The easy way to diversify

An equity mutual fund, index fund or ETF holds many companies across sectors in one purchase. For most people, a fund is the simplest way to get proper diversification, especially with smaller amounts. Single stocks can then be a small extra, not the core.

### Matching money to time

The date you need the money comes first:

| Needed in | Usually fits |
|---|---|
| Within 1 year | Bank savings, money market funds |
| 1 to 3 years | Money market, sovereign income or income funds, National Savings |
| 5+ years | Equity funds, index funds or ETFs, some shares, some gold |

Money for next year's wedding should not be in shares, however good the market looks.

### A sample split by age and goal

These are **illustrative examples only**, not advice. Your own mix depends on your income, family, goals and how you handle losses. A rough old rule of thumb is "100 minus your age in shares", but treat it only as a starting point for thinking.

| Profile | Fixed income and cash | Shares (mostly funds) | Gold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age 25, saving for 15+ years | 30% | 60% | 10% |
| Age 40, children's education in 8 to 10 years | 45% | 45% | 10% |
| Age 58, retiring soon | 70% | 20% | 10% |
| Anyone, goal within 2 years | 90% to 100% | 0% | 0% to 10% |

Your emergency fund sits outside this table. It should already be safe and easy to reach.

If your profile is Shariah-conscious, use the Islamic versions: Islamic money market and income funds, sukuk funds, Islamic equity funds and KMI-30 based ETFs. Lesson 7 covers this.

### Rebalancing

Over time, winners grow and your mix drifts. If shares do very well, a 60% share slice can quietly become 75%, so you are taking more risk than you planned. **Rebalancing** means moving back to your target, once or twice a year or when a slice drifts well beyond it. The cheapest way is to put new money into whatever is underweight, rather than selling.

## A Pakistani Example

Bilal, 30, has Rs 600,000 to invest for at least 10 years, after keeping his emergency fund aside. His first idea: put it all into two bank shares he likes. Then he remembers this lesson.

He picks an illustrative 30 / 60 / 10 split:

| Basket | % | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Money market and income funds | 30% | Rs 180,000 |
| Shares (an equity index fund plus a few stocks from different sectors) | 60% | Rs 360,000 |
| Gold | 10% | Rs 60,000 |

Inside the share slice, he puts Rs 270,000 in a diversified equity fund and Rs 90,000 in three stocks from different sectors, say a cement company, a pharma company and an E&P company (illustrative only).

One year later, made-up results: shares are up 25%, fixed income up 10%, gold down 5%. His mix is now worth:

- Fixed income: 180,000 × 1.10 = Rs 198,000
- Shares: 360,000 × 1.25 = Rs 450,000
- Gold: 60,000 × 0.95 = Rs 57,000
- Total: **Rs 705,000**. Shares are now about 64% of the total.

He directs his next few months of savings into fixed income and gold to bring the mix back to 30 / 60 / 10.

## Common Mistakes

- Owning many shares that are all from the same one or two sectors.
- Putting short-term money into shares because the market is rising.
- Over-diversifying into dozens of tiny holdings you cannot follow. A fund does this job better.
- Never rebalancing, so risk creeps up after a long rally.
- Forgetting what you already hold, such as property, a business or employer shares, when sizing your mix.

## Key Takeaways

- Spread across asset classes, sectors and companies.
- Low-correlation mixes give a smoother ride.
- Decide your split by when you need the money first, then by how much risk you can carry.
- Funds are the easiest way to diversify. Single stocks can be a small extra.
- Rebalance occasionally, preferably with new money. This is education, not personal advice.
