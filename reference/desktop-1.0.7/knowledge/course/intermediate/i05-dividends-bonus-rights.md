## The Big Idea

Companies share their success with owners in three main ways: cash **dividends**, free **bonus shares**, and **rights issues** that let you buy new shares. Each comes with dates you must understand. The biggest lesson: on the day a share goes "ex", its price usually drops by about the value handed out. None of these is free money by itself.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Cash dividends

A **dividend** is part of a company's profit paid to shareholders in cash. The board decides whether to pay one, usually when it announces results.

In Pakistan, dividends are often announced as a **percentage of face value**. **Face value** (or par value) is the nominal value printed on each share, often Rs 10 but not always. So a "25% dividend" on a Rs 10 face value share means Rs 2.50 per share, whatever the market price is. Check the company's notice for its face value.

The company pays the cash straight into the bank account (IBAN) registered with your CDC account, so keep it up to date.

### Dividend yield

**Dividend yield** tells you how much cash you get each year compared with the share price:

**Dividend yield = yearly dividend per share ÷ share price × 100**

A Rs 100 share paying Rs 8 a year has an 8% yield. But a very high yield can be a warning sign: sometimes the price has fallen because the market expects the dividend to be cut.

### Book closure and the ex-date

The company's notice announces a **book closure** period. Shareholders on the register at the close of business on the day before book closure get the payment.

Here is the catch. You only appear on the register after your purchase **settles** in CDC. So you must buy early enough for settlement to finish before the cut-off. The first trading day on which a buyer will no longer get the dividend is the **ex-date**. The last day to buy depends on PSX's settlement cycle, so check the cycle and the company notice.

On PSX's screens and closing rate summary you will see tags next to a symbol on its ex-day:

| Tag | Means | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **XD** | Ex-dividend | Buyers from today don't get the declared cash dividend. |
| **XB** | Ex-bonus | Buyers from today don't get the bonus shares. |
| **XR** | Ex-rights | Buyers from today don't get the right shares. |

### Why the price drops on the ex-date

Before the ex-date, each share carries the right to, say, Rs 5 of cash. After it, the cash goes to the old owners, so the price usually falls by about Rs 5. Value has moved from the share price into bank accounts, minus tax.

So buying just before the ex-date "to catch the dividend" gains nothing after tax and costs.

### Withholding tax on dividends

**Withholding tax** is tax the company deducts from your dividend at source and pays to the government. Rates depend on your filer status and the type of dividend, and change with budgets. Non-filers pay much more. Zakat may also be deducted unless you have filed an exemption. Check FBR's current rate card for figures.

### Bonus shares

A **bonus issue** gives you free extra shares in proportion to what you own. A 10% bonus means 1 new share for every 10 you hold. But no new value is created. The same company is cut into more pieces, so the price adjusts down on the ex-bonus date.

### Rights issues

A **rights issue** is an offer to existing shareholders to buy new shares, usually at a discount, in proportion to their holding. You have three choices:

1. **Subscribe**: pay for the new shares through your broker by the deadline.
2. **Sell the rights**: they trade on PSX for a short window.
3. **Do nothing**: the rights lapse and your ownership share is **diluted** (made smaller).

The share price adjusts down ex-rights, so doing nothing quietly costs you. Ask why the company needs money: growth is a good sign, covering losses a warning.

## A Pakistani Example

Hina, a pharmacist in Rawalpindi, owns 200 shares of a fertilizer company, say Fauji Fertilizer, bought at an illustrative Rs 150. All figures are made up.

**Dividend.** The company declares a dividend of Rs 12 per share for the year.

- Dividend yield on her price: 12 ÷ 150 × 100 = **8%**.
- Gross dividend: 200 × 12 = Rs 2,400.
- If withholding tax were 15% (an example rate), she receives Rs 2,040.
- On the XD day the share opens around Rs 12 lower, other things being equal.

**Bonus.** Next year the company gives a 10% bonus. Hina now holds 220 shares. If the price was Rs 165 before, it adjusts to about 165 ÷ 1.1 = Rs 150. Her value: 200 × 165 = Rs 33,000 before, and 220 × 150 = Rs 33,000 after. Same money, more pieces.

**Rights.** A different company she owns offers 1 right share for every 4 held at Rs 60, when the price is Rs 100. The theoretical price after the issue is (4 × 100 + 60) ÷ 5 = Rs 92. Ignoring the offer would cost her value, so she subscribes or sells her rights.

## Common Mistakes

- Buying right before the ex-date to "get the dividend", then watching the price drop by about the same amount.
- Thinking a bonus issue makes you richer.
- Ignoring a rights letter and letting your stake get diluted.

## Key Takeaways

- Dividend yield = yearly dividend ÷ price × 100. A very high yield deserves a closer look.
- You must be on the register by the book closure cut-off, and settlement takes time.
- XD, XB and XR mark the ex-days. Prices usually drop by about the value given out.
- Dividends are taxed at source; the rate depends on filer status and current law.
- With rights, act: subscribe or sell. Doing nothing dilutes you.
