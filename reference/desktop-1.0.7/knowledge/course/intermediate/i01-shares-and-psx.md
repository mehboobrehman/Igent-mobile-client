## The Big Idea

A **share** is a small slice of ownership in a company. When you buy one, you are not lending money. You become a part-owner, so you share in the company's profits and its problems. In Pakistan, most shares change hands on the **Pakistan Stock Exchange (PSX)**, a marketplace where buyers and sellers meet through licensed brokers.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### A share is part of a business

Picture a big Lahore bakery split into 1,000 equal pieces. Own 10 pieces and you own 1% of it. If the bakery earns more, your slice is worth more; in a bad year, less.

A listed company works the same way, split into millions or even billions of shares. As a shareholder you may get:

- A share of profits paid out as cash, called a **dividend** (Lesson 5 covers this).
- A rising share price if the business grows.
- The right to vote at the company's annual general meeting.

Nothing is promised: prices can fall and dividends can stop.

### Listed companies

A **listed company** has gone through rules set by PSX and the **Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP)**, the regulator, so that its shares can trade on the exchange. Listed companies must publish results and announce big news, such as dividends, on the PSX website. That public information is what makes it possible for ordinary people to judge them.

### PSX: one exchange, fully electronic

PSX is Pakistan's only stock exchange, formed in 2016 when the Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad exchanges merged. Trading is fully electronic.

The trading day follows a pattern:

| Session | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Pre-open** | Orders are collected and matched at a single opening price. |
| **Continuous trading** | The main session. Buy and sell orders match one by one all day. |
| **Post-close** | A short session after the close. |

The market runs Monday to Friday, with a break on Friday for Jumma prayers. Hours change in Ramadan and the market closes on public holidays. Timings change from time to time, so check the trading-hours page on the PSX website rather than memorising them.

### Who does what

You cannot trade on PSX by yourself. Several players work together:

| Player | Role in plain words |
|---|---|
| **Broker** | A firm licensed by SECP and holding a PSX trading right. You place orders through its app or website. |
| **CDC (Central Depository Company)** | Holds your shares electronically, in an account in your name. Think of it as a bank vault for shares. |
| **NCCPL (National Clearing Company)** | Clears and settles trades, gives you a unique investor number, and calculates and collects capital gains tax. |
| **SECP** | The regulator that licenses and supervises brokers and the market. |

Your shares sit in your own **CDC sub-account** (opened by your broker), not in the broker's pocket. Lesson 2 shows how to set it up.

### Settlement: when the deal is really done

When you press Buy and your order matches, the deal is agreed, but money and shares have not swapped yet. That swap is called **settlement**. It happens a fixed number of business days after the trade, written as "T+1", "T+2" and so on (T means trade day). PSX has shortened this cycle over the years. Check PSX or your broker for the current cycle. For you it means: pay for your buys in time, and only sell shares you already hold.

### Market capitalisation: how big is a company?

**Market capitalisation (market cap)** is the total value the market puts on a company:

**Market cap = share price × total number of shares**

A share price alone tells you nothing about size. A Rs 50 share can belong to a far bigger company than a Rs 900 share, if it has many more shares. Companies are often grouped as large cap, mid cap and small cap. Large caps usually trade more and are easier to buy and sell.

## A Pakistani Example

Bilal runs a mobile accessories shop in Lahore. His friend says, "Buy shares in a cement company. It's only Rs 80!" Bilal decides to learn first.

He looks at two made-up companies (example numbers only):

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Share price | Rs 80 | Rs 800 |
| Number of shares | 1,000 million | 50 million |
| Market cap | Rs 80,000 million | Rs 40,000 million |

The "cheap" Rs 80 share belongs to the company that is twice as big. Price per share says nothing about cheap or expensive. What matters is what you get for the price, which you will learn in a later level.

Later Bilal buys 50 shares of a large listed company, say Lucky Cement, at an illustrative Rs 800. He pays about Rs 40,000 plus charges. After settlement he gets an SMS from CDC confirming that 50 shares are now in his own CDC sub-account. He owns a very small slice of a real business.

## Common Mistakes

- Thinking a low share price means a stock is "cheap". Price must be compared with the company's size and profits.
- Treating shares like a savings account. Shares carry no guaranteed return and can fall sharply.
- Handing money to someone who promises to "trade on PSX for you". Only licensed brokers can place orders, and your shares should sit in your own CDC account.
- Memorising market timings or settlement rules from old posts. Rules change, so check PSX.

## Key Takeaways

- A share is part-ownership of a business, with a share in its profits and risks.
- PSX is Pakistan's single, electronic stock exchange, regulated by SECP.
- Brokers place your orders, CDC holds your shares, and NCCPL settles trades.
- Market cap (price × shares) shows a company's size. The share price alone does not.
- This lesson is education, not advice. Check current rules on the PSX website.
