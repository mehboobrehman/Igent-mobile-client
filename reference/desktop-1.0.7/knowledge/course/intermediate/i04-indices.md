## The Big Idea

An **index** is a single number that shows how a basket of shares is doing. When the news says "the market rose today", it usually means the KSE-100 index went up. Indices help you read the market's mood quickly and, just as important, give you a fair yardstick to judge your own investing.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### What an index is

Imagine checking the price of 100 different items in Lahore's markets every day and combining them into one number. If the number goes up, prices overall went up, even if a few items got cheaper. A stock index does the same with shares.

An index does not own anything. It is a calculation. You cannot buy "the KSE-100" directly, but you can buy a fund or ETF that copies an index.

### Free float: counting only shares that really trade

Many Pakistani companies have big owners, such as sponsor families, parent companies or the government, who rarely sell. Their shares are not really available to the market. **Free float** is the part of a company's shares that is actually available for trading by the public.

PSX's main indices are **free-float weighted**. That means a company's influence on the index depends on the market value of its free-float shares, not its full size. A huge company with a tiny free float counts for less than you might expect.

The bigger a company's free-float value, the more it moves the index. So a few large companies can push the whole index up or down on their own.

### The main PSX indices

| Index | What it contains |
|---|---|
| **KSE-100** | The benchmark. Large companies by free-float market cap, with representation from different sectors. |
| **KSE-30** | The 30 most liquid large companies. Big moves in this index trigger a market-wide halt. |
| **KMI-30** | The 30 most liquid **Shariah-compliant** companies, with a cap on any single company's weight. |
| **PSX-KMI All Share** | All Shariah-compliant listed companies. |
| **All Share** | Every listed company. |

There are also sector indices (for example banks or oil and gas) and indices that ETFs track. PSX reviews and reshuffles index members from time to time, so a company can join or leave an index. The PSX indices page and Data Portal show current members.

One useful difference: the KMI-30 contains no conventional banks, while the KSE-100 does. So on a day when bank shares rally, the KSE-100 often does better than the KMI-30.

### Points versus percent

The news loves points: "KSE-100 jumps 1,200 points!" Points alone mean little, because the index level changes over the years. Always turn points into percent:

**% change = points moved ÷ previous level × 100**

A 1,200-point move is a big deal when the index is at 20,000 (6%) and a modest one when it is at 120,000 (1%). Igent's Dashboard shows the change in percent for exactly this reason.

### The index is not the economy

The KSE-100 leans heavily on a few large sectors, historically banks, oil and gas exploration, fertilizer, cement and power. It can rise while many small companies fall, and it does not measure how ordinary families are doing. Treat it as "how big listed companies are doing", nothing more.

### Comparing yourself with the index

An index is your **benchmark**, the result you could have got by simply owning the whole basket through an index fund. If your hand-picked shares earned 10% in a year when the KSE-100 earned 15%, you did worse than doing nothing clever.

Tips for a fair comparison:

- Compare percentages over the same period.
- Use the right index. Shariah portfolios and Islamic funds should be compared with the KMI-30, not the KSE-100.
- Include dividends on both sides where you can. A price-only comparison can mislead. The PSX indices page explains what each index measures.
- Include your costs. Your return after charges is the one that counts.

## A Pakistani Example

Ayesha and her brother Hamza both invested Rs 200,000 at the start of a year. All numbers here are made up.

- Ayesha bought five Shariah-compliant stocks. A year later her portfolio, including dividends, is worth Rs 224,000. Her return: 24,000 ÷ 200,000 = **12%**.
- Hamza bought three bank shares and two cement shares. His portfolio is worth Rs 230,000. His return: **15%**.

Hamza brags. But over the same year, imagine the KSE-100 rose 20% and the KMI-30 rose 10%.

| Investor | Return | Right benchmark | Benchmark return | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ayesha | 12% | KMI-30 | 10% | Beat her benchmark |
| Hamza | 15% | KSE-100 | 20% | Fell behind |

Hamza made more rupees but did worse than a simple KSE-100 index fund. Ayesha did better than the halal benchmark. Comparing with the right index tells the real story.

## Common Mistakes

- Getting excited or scared by points instead of percent.
- Judging a Shariah portfolio against the KSE-100 (or a conventional one against the KMI-30).
- Thinking the KSE-100 rising means every share or the whole economy is doing well.
- Comparing your return including dividends with an index return that excludes them, or the other way round.
- Using an old list of index members. Members change after PSX reviews.

## Key Takeaways

- An index is one number tracking a basket of shares, weighted by free-float market value.
- KSE-100 is the main benchmark; KMI-30 is the Shariah benchmark; KSE-30 drives market halts.
- Always read moves in percent, not points.
- Use the right index as your yardstick and compare after costs.
- Index members change. Check PSX for current lists.
