## The Big Idea

Every buy should come with a reason you can write down. An **investment thesis** is that reason: what you expect to happen, why you expect it, and what would tell you that you were wrong. If you cannot write it in five lines, you are not investing yet. You are guessing.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Why bother writing it down?

Your memory is kind to you. When a stock goes up, you will "remember" that you always knew. When it goes down, you will invent new reasons to keep holding. A written thesis stops both tricks. It turns a feeling ("this looks cheap") into a claim you can check later.

A good thesis is **testable**. That means a stranger could read it in six months and say "yes, that happened" or "no, it did not".

### The five parts

| Part | Question it answers | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expectation** | What do I think will happen? | "It will go up." | "Profit per share grows about 15% a year for two years." |
| **Reason** | Why do I think so? | "Everyone is buying." | "New capacity is running and costs are falling." |
| **Evidence** | What facts support it today? | "A YouTuber said so." | "Last two quarterly results showed sales up and margins wider." |
| **Catalyst** | What event could make the market see it? | none | "Next annual result and dividend in about three months." |
| **Thesis-breaker** | What would prove me wrong? | none | "If margins fall two quarters in a row, I exit." |

A **catalyst** is an event that could close the gap between the price and the value you see. Think results announcements, a dividend, a policy rate decision, a budget, or a new plant starting up. A thesis with no catalyst can still work, but it may take much longer.

The **thesis-breaker** is the most important line. Writing it *before* you buy protects you from **confirmation bias**, the habit of noticing only facts that agree with you.

### Evidence, not vibes

Good evidence comes from things you learned earlier in this course:

- **Results:** sales, margins, core EPS, cash flow.
- **Valuation:** P/E, P/B, dividend yield against history and peers.
- **Sector and macro:** what the policy rate, rupee, oil or budget mean for this business.
- **Price already paid:** what does the market already expect? A great company at a crazy price can be a bad investment.

Bad evidence: tips in WhatsApp groups, "it fell a lot so it must bounce", or "my friend made money on it".

### Compare with the safe choice

Every thesis needs a line on expected return. Then ask: does it clearly beat what I could earn almost risk-free, after tax? You will do this properly in lesson e05. For now, just write a rough number.

### Your 5-line thesis template

Copy this into your notes or journal:

```text
1. I expect: ______ (what happens, by roughly when)
2. Because: ______ (the business or economic reason)
3. Evidence today: ______ (2-3 facts I checked myself)
4. Catalyst: ______ (event, approximate date)
5. I am wrong if: ______ (specific signs that break the thesis)
Rough expected return per year: ___%  vs safe rate after tax: ___%
```

## A Pakistani Example

*All numbers below are made-up round figures for teaching, not real data.*

Sara is looking at **Engro Fertilizers (EFERT)**. In this example the share price is Rs 200, last year's dividend was Rs 20 per share, and core EPS was Rs 25.

Her first try: *"Fertilizer is a safe sector and EFERT pays good dividends."* That is a feeling, not a thesis. Nothing in it could be proven wrong.

Her second try:

```text
1. I expect: EFERT keeps paying about Rs 20 a year in dividends for the next 2 years,
   and the price drifts up slowly as profits hold.
2. Because: urea demand is steady, the plant is running well, and debt is low.
3. Evidence today: last 4 quarters of results show stable margins; payout about 80% of EPS;
   cash flow covers the dividend.
4. Catalyst: annual results and final dividend in about 3 months.
5. I am wrong if: the dividend is cut below Rs 14, or a gas price change squeezes margins
   for two quarters with no price increase passed on.
Rough expected return: dividend yield 10% + price gain ~4% = ~14% a year before tax.
```

Now Sara has something useful. In three months she checks the result. Dividend Rs 20? The thesis holds. Dividend Rs 12 because of a gas cost shock? Her line 5 says she is wrong, and she already decided what to do about it, before emotions arrive.

Notice what the thesis does *not* say: "The price will hit Rs 300." Price targets invite anchoring. Business outcomes are easier to check.

## Common Mistakes

- **No thesis-breaker.** Without one, every bad result becomes "temporary" and you never sell.
- **Price as the thesis.** "It will go to Rs 300" is a hope, not a reason.
- **Borrowed evidence.** Using someone else's tip instead of facts you checked yourself.
- **Ignoring the price paid.** A good company is not automatically a good buy at any price.
- **Changing the thesis after the fact.** If the reason changes, write a new thesis and judge it fresh.

## Key Takeaways

- A thesis has five parts: expectation, reason, evidence, catalyst and thesis-breaker.
- It must be testable, so you can tell later whether you were right.
- Write what would prove you wrong *before* you buy.
- Always note a rough expected return and compare it with the safe rate after tax.
- Igent's idea cards follow the same shape, so read them as practice in judging theses.
