## The Big Idea

Picking a good idea is only half the job. The other half is deciding **how much** to put in. A great idea with too much money can still wreck you if you are wrong, and you *will* be wrong sometimes. Good sizing means no single mistake can sink your plan.

## Explain It Like I'm New

### Never bet the farm

A **position** is how much you hold of one stock or fund. **Position sizing** is choosing that amount on purpose.

Why it matters: big losses are very hard to climb out of.

| Loss | Gain needed to get back to even |
|---|---|
| 10% | about 11% |
| 25% | about 33% |
| 50% | 100% |
| 75% | 300% |

So the first rule is defensive: keep each bet small enough that a bad outcome is survivable. Before any of this, your emergency fund and money needed soon stay out. Size from **investable cash** only.

### Step 1: Pick a maximum loss per idea

Decide the most you are willing to lose on any single idea, as a percent of your whole portfolio. Many careful investors use something like 1% to 3%. This is a personal choice, not a law, so pick what lets you sleep.

**Max loss per idea (Rs) = portfolio value x max loss %**

### Step 2: Estimate the bad-case fall

Ask honestly: in a really bad year, how far could this fall? This is the **downside**.

| Holding (examples only) | Possible bad-year fall to plan for |
|---|---|
| Money market fund | very small |
| Diversified equity fund or index ETF | 20% to 30%, sometimes more |
| Large, liquid PSX stock | 30% to 50% |
| Small, thinly traded stock | 50% or more |

These are rough planning numbers, not forecasts. Check the holding's own bad years.

### Step 3: Do the maths

**Position size = max loss per idea / bad-case fall**

If you can lose Rs 20,000 and the stock might fall 40%, the position is Rs 20,000 / 0.40 = Rs 50,000. If it does fall 40%, you lose Rs 20,000, exactly what you agreed to.

### Step 4: Adjust for conviction

**Conviction** means how confident you are in the thesis, based on evidence, not feelings. Igent's house rule of thumb works well:

- **High conviction, liquid, diversified:** up to your full cap.
- **Medium conviction, liquid large stock:** about half the cap.
- **Low conviction, or illiquid/volatile:** a quarter of the cap or less, or just watch.

Igent has a setting, **Largest Single Idea (% of Cash)**, that idea cards never go above. Set it to your own limit.

### Step 5: Check liquidity

**Liquidity** means how easily you can sell without moving the price. Some PSX shares trade very little each day. In a panic, buyers vanish, and a share can sit at its lower price limit with almost no buyers. Before buying, look at typical daily volume. If your order would be a big slice of a normal day's trading, cut the size. Thin stocks deserve smaller positions even if you love the story.

### Stop-loss vs thesis-break exits

A **stop-loss** is a price at which you plan to sell to limit loss. It removes emotion, but in jumpy PSX names it can trigger on noise, and on a gap down your sale can happen far below the stop.

A **thesis-break exit** means you sell when the *reason* you bought is broken (the line 5 from lesson e01), whatever the price.

A practical mix: use a price level as a **review point** ("if it falls 25%, I re-read my thesis that day") and the thesis-breaker as the real exit rule. Your sizing already limited the damage, so you do not need a hair-trigger stop.

### Start partial, add later

You do not have to buy it all at once. Buy half now, and the rest later only if the thesis still holds. And count what you already own: if you hold two bank stocks, a third bank is not a new idea. It is a bigger bank bet.

### Sizing checklist

```text
[ ] Emergency fund and near-term money excluded

[ ] Below my cap (Largest Single Idea %)
[ ] Liquid enough to exit in a bad week
[ ] Counts existing holdings in this name and sector
[ ] First buy: ___ now, rest in steps
```

## A Pakistani Example

*All numbers are made-up round figures for teaching, not real data or advice.*

Bilal has Rs 1,000,000 of investable cash (his emergency fund is separate). He sets his max loss per idea at 2%, which is **Rs 20,000**. His cap is 10% (Rs 100,000).

He has two ideas:

| | Meezan Bank (MEBL), example | A small textile stock, example |
|---|---|---|
| Conviction | Medium | Low |
| Bad-case fall | 40% | 60% |
| Size by maths | 20,000 / 0.40 = Rs 50,000 | 20,000 / 0.60 = Rs 33,333 |
| Conviction adjustment | Half the cap = Rs 50,000, fits | Quarter of cap = Rs 25,000 |
| Liquidity | Trades well | Thin, wide spreads |
| Final plan | Rs 50,000: Rs 25,000 now, Rs 25,000 later | Rs 15,000 or just watch |

For the textile stock, low conviction cut the size to Rs 25,000 and thin trading cut it again. Bilal decides to watch it instead.

Now imagine the worst for MEBL: it falls 40%. Bilal loses Rs 20,000, or 2% of his money. Painful, not ruinous. His plan survives and he can think clearly.

## Common Mistakes

- **Sizing by excitement.** The idea you love most is not automatically the one that deserves the most money.
- **Forgetting the downside.** Treating a volatile small stock like a fund.
- **Ignoring liquidity.** Buying a thin stock you cannot sell when you need to.
- **Hidden concentration.** Five stocks from one sector act like one big bet.

## Key Takeaways

- Big losses need much bigger gains to recover, so protect against them first.
- Size = max loss per idea / bad-case fall, then adjust for conviction and liquidity.
- Never go above your own cap. Start partial and add only if the thesis holds.
- Use price drops as review points and thesis-breaks as exit rules.
